Article of the Week

The Tyranny of a Narrow Strait

By Abhishek Kumar, published 28 November 2026

Every economics student learns that oil is a commodity. Every finance minister learns, sometimes at a very inconvenient moment, that it is also a weapon.

Brent crude touched $110 a barrel last Friday as Iran stepped up attacks on shipping near the Strait of Hormuz, with at least nine commercial vessels struck in a single week. Flows through the strait are running roughly 43% below their level before the war began in February, and although the G7 has released 100 million barrels from emergency stocks, traders noticed, shrugged, and kept the risk premium.

For India the arithmetic is unforgiving. We buy most of the crude we refine from abroad, and a large share of it sails past Iranian coastal batteries. A sustained rise in the price of a barrel shows up in the current account, in the fertiliser subsidy, in the diesel bill of a truck operator in Nagpur and, a few weeks later, in the price of onions in Delhi.

For the better part of two decades, India thought about energy security in fairly simple terms, which amounted to buying as many barrels as the economy needed, from as many sellers as would deal with it, at a price the budget could bear. Judged by that standard the approach has worked, since refiners loaded up on discounted Russian crude while Western capitals grumbled, Gulf producers were courted with investment pledges, and this week Marco Rubio, America’s secretary of state, arrived calling India a refining superpower. The difficulty is that each of these friendships comes with conditions that can shift whenever Washington or Moscow changes its mind.

What has happened in the power sector has drawn less attention, though it may matter more in the long run. About 54% of India’s installed generating capacity is now non-fossil, and in the first seven months of this year wind and solar met a third of electricity demand, even if coal still supplies the larger share. BloombergNEF estimates that a transition driven by economics alone could cut the country’s energy import bill from 5.3% of GDP last year to 3.1% by 2035, a degree of protection few defence budgets could buy, because a solar park outside Jodhpur is hard to blockade and nobody charges war-risk insurance on sunshine.

None of this makes the problem disappear, particularly as the International Energy Agency expects electricity demand to rise by 6.4% a year until the end of the decade. Most of the panels, cells and processed minerals India depends on are made in China, so reliance on one strait is partly being exchanged for reliance on one country, and installed capacity says little about whether the lights will stay on at eight on a May evening, when every air conditioner in Delhi is running.

If I had to pick where policy should go next, I would start with storage, because India’s strategic petroleum reserves are small by international standards and batteries are being tendered far faster than they are being commissioned, even though a recent round-the-clock tender for clean power cleared at a cheap ₹5.25 a unit. Pricing deserves equal attention, since holding pump prices down and leaving state oil companies to absorb the losses merely moves the cost onto their balance sheets, where it quietly weakens any incentive to switch fuels. Nuclear power will need patience as well. The Shanti Act has opened the sector to private and foreign firms, and the government is aiming for 100 gigawatts by 2047 against fewer than nine today, although India has announced ambitious nuclear targets before and found that building the plants was a different matter.

Most students at FMS will end up in boardrooms rather than ministries, which is precisely why this debate should interest them, as the cost of fuel and the risk of a shipping lane closing now feed into decisions ranging from supplier contracts to capital spending. Firms that build these risks into their planning early will fare much better than those that wait for the next crisis to force the question.
